Task: score 0-5 how much this answer READS AS concrete, specific proof of past delivery, based

Critical rule — do NOT judge truthfulness: you have no way to verify whether anything the bidder
states is real, accurate, or fabricated, and it is not your job to guess. Never lower a score
because a cited example seems "unverified", "unconfirmed", "fictional", "made up", or because you
don't personally recognise the named project/client. The bidder is the one who knows whether
their own example is real — your only job is to judge HOW it is written: does it read with the
specificity and tense of genuine evidence, or does it read as a vague, generic, future-tense
promise. Take every factual claim in the answer at face value.

What makes writing read as strong evidence (drawn from real UK public-sector evaluator feedback —
see the worked examples below):
- Named, specific detail: a project, client, contract, or context identified concretely (e.g.
  "on the Riverside contract") rather than vaguely (e.g. "on a similar project").
- NAMING a mechanism is not, by itself, strong evidence. A real evaluator consistently marks
  responses down for citing a named initiative, model, or office (e.g. "our delivery academy",
  "our value realisation framework") but then failing to explain HOW it actually operated IN
  PRACTICE, or what it measurably achieved. The gap between "named it" and "proved it" is the
  single biggest thing separating a winning answer from a losing one in real evaluator feedback —
  score strong evidence only when the mechanism is both named AND explained in operation AND tied
  to a measured outcome, not for the naming alone.
- Quantified or measurable detail: numbers, percentages, timeframes, concrete results (e.g.
  "reduced onboarding time by 40% within 3 weeks") rather than vague claims (e.g. "significantly
  improved", "very successful").
- PAST TENSE, stated as something that was actually done and achieved ("we identified... we
  delivered... we reduced...") — not FUTURE TENSE or CONDITIONAL phrasing describing what would
  or could be done ("we will ensure...", "this could be converted into..."). A future-tense or
  conditional promise, however confident or detailed, can never score above 2 — anyone can
  promise or hypothesise; only a definite past-tense claim reads as a track record.

Worked examples (generic, illustrative — judge the actual answer above on its own merits, not by

sounding mechanism named without the practical detail or the number behind it.

Rules:
- 5: specific, named, quantified, definite past-tense claim of having actually done this before,
  with the mechanism explained in operation AND a measurable outcome stated.
- 3-4: a named, past-tense mechanism with at least some operational detail or a partial outcome,

  number.
- 1-2: names a mechanism or initiative but doesn't explain how it operated or what it achieved
  (Example B) — a label, not proof — even though it isn't vague in the abstract sense.
- 0: vague, generic, future-tense, or conditional, with no named mechanism at all (Example C).
- Judge only the wording and tense of the answer as given — never its truthfulness, plausibility,
